This is just a quick and dirty article on general improvement in Tetris or similar games. Not necessarily any particular kind of Tetris, as there are countless variants. But these general concepts you should be able to apply to many different kinds of Tetris, whether you play Guideline, TGM, NES/Classic, Sega or something else.

# First up: Have fun!

I think learning to improve in Tetris should come with a prerequisite that you actually enjoy playing. If you try and improve in something and don't enjoy it or go into it with a bad mindset, you are bound to improve at a slower pace than if you're just vibing and taking things in stride, step by step. It's a video game, after all, though this advice goes for anything in life. If you don't enjoy it, in my personal opinion there is little reason to continue learning and improving, unless it's actually a necessity in your life (such as something job related).

# Learn how the pieces fit together and behave!

Just experiment a bit, place the pieces around, and see what kind of things you can do with your stack. Eventually, you may be able to see patterns for where to place certain pieces in a way that doesn't jumble up the stack. This is much easier said than done of course. What you can do here is either try to brute force it, or you can watch better players (you can also slow down their videos and watch in slow motion in case it's too fast). Just analyse what they do and try to derive why they do it - is it a safe play? Could they be setting up for a move later on? Is it a move that, at first glance, *looks* wrong but ends up being a way to fix an issue in the stack?

This is an extremely deep topic and something you will keep learning and improving on many years down the line. As you learn to discover more common ways of placing pieces, you will automatically recognise patterns faster and just automatically play faster! It's also important that, depending on what kind of Tetris version you play, moves such as unique Spins/Twists may be a thing, which themselves are unique patterns, but can help diversify how you can play in a more "safe" way with more piece placement options! Do also note that Spins/Twists will be different depending on what kind of Tetris game you play and not all of them will work in other engines (or at all, even!)

# Use both rotation directions!

The games usually give you two ways to rotate, use them both! If you rotate thrice instead of once, that's two unnecessary inputs - and if you're going for speed, such as a 40 Lines game, this will add up over time. It's a pretty small and easily explained thing, but does quite a lot.

Important: In some cases, doing multiple rotations can be beneficial, in particular when you play TGM! The term "triple rotate" exists for a good reason and can allow for more flexible placement of pieces at faster speeds. Same goes for, for example, doing a 180 to set a T flat, letting it cross a 1 or 2 block wide gap and rotating it again into position, like on top of a S/Z for example.

# Learn how the pieces spawn!

This is a pretty large topic in itself, but is one of three key components to playing fast (I'll cover the other two later on). You want to get a general idea in what orientation, and in what columns the pieces end up spawning. That way, you have a better grasp of how exactly you have to move your pieces to get them to just the right spot, such as "Do I have to move the T-Piece twice to the left? Yes I do, because I know where it spawns!". Something along those lines anyway. This leads into the next, possibly the biggest key component, and the most daunting one:

# Finesse! Minimize inputs needed for each piece.

This sounds easy on paper but it's a huge deal, and it's different for every variant of Tetris out there. Every piece will spawn differently in a different variant, and they will behave differently when rotating and moving as a whole.

Depending on your variant, there are different tutorials for this that go very in depth. But a few important notes are, for example: "The I piece has to go into the 8th column! To save inputs, I can just not rotate it, move it all the way to the right, rotate once and then lock the piece!" As opposed to "I will rotate the piece, move all the way to the right, press left twice and then lock the piece". Doing it the first way will save two inputs, for example.

This ties into a different thing too, which is indecisiveness. Think about where to place the piece before doing any sort of movement if possible, and then use the proper minimum amount of inputs needed to place that piece. Having a ghost piece *can* help with learning certain patterns and moves but try to think about where to place a piece before moving it, and if you made a decision, try your best to not reconsider. At faster speeds, being indecisive can very likely end a run.

I recommend you look up Finesse for your kind of game though. So if you play Tetris Effect, you'd want Guideline Finesse, or if you play TGM, you'd want TGM finesse. Quite a few variants have the finesse documented though and will tell you exactly what to do to get your piece to the right location in the right orientation with the least amount of inputs - which is the ultimate goal. The less inputs, the less strained your hands will be, and the faster you can push your speeds. This is crucial for high level play. You can play fast without finesse, but you're unnecessarily hindering your own speed. Just one more thing to go that will help:

# Play ahead. Use the preview box(es)!

Quite a bunch of players end up being able to find patterns to place pieces in so fast, they can play the game ahead in their brain. As example, the current piece is a Z piece, and the next is a T, followed by an I. In their brain, they can go "I can place the Z in a way to allow me to fire off a T-Spin Double, and after that remove 4 lines of my garbage with the I piece", or even just "An I piece is coming up in 4 pieces, I'm ready for a 4 line clear when it's here". Planning ahead and learning to strategize for a clean playfield allows you to go even faster. Rest assured, if you'd hide the next pieces for any top player, their reaction time would suddenly be a lot, lot worse.

The next one is also important and something that can be applied to any game:

# Study your mistakes, and study other people!

This one's a bigger deal than you might think. Record your gameplay and look back at it. What other placements could you have done that would be more accomodating? Is there one placement that just royally screwed you? Could you have done something to amend it a bit? These are important questions to ask to yourself. Though, of course, it's important to not be too harsh to yourself when doing this. You yourself are your worst critic, after all. Don't get discouraged if you see many "mistakes", learning Tetris takes quite a bit of time. It's a great way to iron out any bad practices you may do such as unnecessary rotations.

But also watching other people play and deducing why they did certain placements in certain ways may help yourself, and you may end up incorporating certain things from watching other people play.

# Take some time to learn the terminology

This is just to make sure that you understand what other guides or people may be talking about. Usually those themselves will come with some sort of glossary, but it doesn't hurt to just look them up yourself.

# The bottom line

That's about all I can think of that the time. Of course, depending on what variation of Tetris you play, there are other important things you may want to learn. In particular for guideline further down the line, Setups and general ideas behind certain setups become extremely important to apply pressure on your opponent faster, which in turn circles back to the whole "pattern recognition" thing that was mentioned earlier. Or for TGM, 20G play (where the pieces spawn at the bottom of the floor) is extremely crucial and handles quite differently from regular Tetris. Depending on what version you play, there will be other important resources for these games that explain these things in great detail.

As such, one final wisdom: Telling people to "play more" is shitty advice. Spite is the greatest motivator, and I mainly wrote this because it's *always* possible to give better advice than just "play more", even if you don't know how good a player is.

Toodles!
